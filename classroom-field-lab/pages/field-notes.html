Classroom Field Notes

Short reflections from real teaching problems.

Each note is deliberately practical: what happened, what I noticed, what I tried, what worked, what I would change, and the AI prompt or tool idea connected to it.

Graph interpretation

Students read the line, but not the story

What happened in class: Several students could calculate gradient but could not explain what the changing gradient meant physically.

What I noticed: They treated the graph as a math object first and a motion story second.

What I tried: I added a prediction step before calculation: describe the motion in one sentence, then estimate the sign and size of the gradient.

What worked: Students began arguing about meaning before reaching for formulas.

What I would change next time: Use two wrong motion stories and ask students to reject one with evidence from the graph.

AI prompt or tool idea: Generate three graph stories with one subtle misconception in each, then create a quick diagnostic question.

Forces

The force diagram looked correct until the explanation began

What happened in class: Students drew the right arrows but still explained motion as if a forward force must be larger whenever an object moves forward.

What I noticed: The diagram had become a drawing routine, not a reasoning tool.

What I tried: I asked students to write one sentence for each arrow: source, direction, and what would change if it disappeared.

What worked: The source question exposed invented forces quickly.

What I would change next time: Add peer checking with a simple rule: no source, no force.

AI prompt or tool idea: Create a set of force diagrams with one hidden error and ask students to diagnose the source problem.

Assessment

Feedback needs to arrive before the next mistake hardens

What happened in class: Marking showed repeated errors, but the full feedback cycle was too slow to affect the next lesson.

What I noticed: I needed faster categorization, not faster typing.

What I tried: I grouped responses into misconception bands and used AI to draft concise feedback stems for each band.

What worked: Feedback stayed specific because the categories came from actual student work.

What I would change next time: Build a reusable feedback table by topic.

AI prompt or tool idea: Turn anonymized student errors into feedback bands, re-teaching prompts, and one follow-up question per band.
